Rosa Delgado was the seventh nanny in four months, and the housekeeper made sure she knew it within the first five minutes.

"The last one lasted nine days," Mrs. Pruitt said, leading her through a hallway lined with paintings that probably cost more than Rosa's entire neighborhood. "Mr. Ashford doesn't tolerate mistakes. And the child — well. You'll see."

The child was sitting at the end of a long glass desk in her father's study, drawing. Six years old, dark curls, a yellow cardigan buttoned wrong. She didn't look up.

"Lily hasn't spoken since the accident," Mrs. Pruitt said, lowering her voice as if the girl might hear. "Not a single word. The doctors say it's grief."

Rosa nodded. She had grown up with a deaf younger brother. She knew what it was like to be talked about as if you weren't in the room.

She sat down across from Lily without saying anything and picked up a crayon.

For twenty minutes they drew in silence. Rosa drew a cat. Lily drew a house with a red door. Rosa drew a sun. Lily drew three people standing in front of the house — two tall, one small.

Then Rosa noticed Lily's left hand under the edge of the desk. The small fingers were moving, quick and precise, making the same shapes again and again.

Not random fidgeting. Letters.

*M-A-R-C-U-S.*

Rosa's crayon stopped mid-stroke. Lily glanced up, met her eyes for the first time — and saw that Rosa had understood.
