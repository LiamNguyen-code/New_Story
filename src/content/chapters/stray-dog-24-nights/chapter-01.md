The first night, Maya Torres almost didn't see him. He was a shape at the edge of the parking lot lights, gray against gray asphalt, with one ear torn and a long scar running down his shoulder.

It was 2:07 a.m. The pharmacy behind her had just gone dark. Her feet ached, her phone was at four percent, and she still had half a turkey sandwich in her bag that she'd been too busy to eat.

She looked at the dog. The dog looked at her.

"Okay," she said quietly, unwrapping the sandwich and setting it on the curb. "But I'm not petting you."

She got into her car and locked the doors. In the rearview mirror, she watched him wait until her engine started before he crept forward to eat.

The second night, he was there again. And the third. By the end of the first week, Maya had started packing two sandwiches.

She named him Ghost, because he never made a sound and because he disappeared the moment her headlights came on. He never let her touch him. But he always sat in the same spot — right beside her car — until she was safely inside.

Her coworker Dana thought she was crazy. "That dog could have rabies. Or fleas. Or a whole gang of other dogs waiting to rob you."

"He's lonely," Maya said. "So am I."

She didn't say the rest out loud: that since her divorce, the twenty steps from the pharmacy door to her car were the loneliest part of her day, and that somehow, with Ghost there, they weren't anymore.
